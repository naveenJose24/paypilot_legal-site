Expense Tracking for NRIs and Expats: One Budget Across Two Countries

If you earn in dirhams and still pay for things in India, your money lives in two places. The trick is not more detail. It is deciding which movements are spending and which are just money moving between your own accounts.

Quick answer

Keep three groups: money spent living in the UAE, money spent in India (EMIs, family support, bills), and transfers between your own accounts. Only the first two are expenses. A remittance into your own NRE account is a transfer, while a payment to your parents for household costs is an expense. Track both currencies as they happen, and note the rate on each remittance.

The three money streams

StreamExamplesHow to treat it
UAE living costsRent, groceries, transport, phone, card spendingExpenses in AED
India commitmentsHome loan EMI, insurance premiums, family support, property tax, school fees for children in IndiaExpenses in INR, or in AED if paid directly from the UAE
Moving your own moneyRemittance to your NRE or NRO account, card bill payments, moving savingsTransfers, not spending

Mixing these up is the most common reason expat budgets look wrong. Count a AED 5,000 transfer to your own NRE account as spending and your month looks AED 5,000 worse than it was. Then count the EMI paid from that account as well, and the same money is counted twice.

Recording remittances and exchange rates

For each remittance, note three things: the AED that left your UAE account, the INR that arrived, and any fee. The effective rate is INR received divided by AED sent, fee included. Over a year this tells you whether one exchange house or app consistently gives you a better deal, which is often worth more than switching a subscription.

Do not try to convert every Indian expense back into AED at the day's rate. Record INR spending in INR, and compare totals per currency. When you need one combined number, convert monthly totals at the average rate you actually got on your remittances.

Getting Indian bank alerts while you live abroad

Automatic tracking depends on the alert reaching your phone. Many NRIs keep an Indian number for OTPs, on a second SIM or eSIM. If that number is active on your phone, Indian bank SMS can be captured the same way as UAE ones. If the Indian SIM sits in a drawer, those alerts never arrive, and you will need to rely on the bank's app notifications or log Indian payments by hand.

Some Indian banks have also stopped sending SMS for small UPI payments and send email instead, so small India-side spending is the part most likely to go missing.

Trips home

A month in India can undo a careful UAE budget: flights, gifts, family events and a lot of small UPI and cash spending. Treat the trip as a project with its own budget. Set aside a monthly amount for it in the UAE (see the Dubai salary budget guide), then log trip spending in INR while you are there and compare the total with what you saved.

Monthly NRI checklist

  1. Confirm salary received in AED.
  2. Record each remittance with AED sent, INR received and fee.
  3. Check India EMIs and premiums were paid, and that the account they come from has enough balance.
  4. Review UAE spending against your budget.
  5. Note any money lent to family or friends, and repayments received.

Questions about NRE versus NRO accounts, or tax in either country, depend on your situation; ask a qualified adviser. This guide is only about keeping clear records.

Frequently asked questions

Is money sent to my own NRE account an expense?

No. It is a transfer between your own accounts. It becomes spending only when it is used to pay for something.

Is money sent to my parents an expense?

If it is support for their household costs, yes, record it as family support. If it is a loan you expect back, record it as money lent instead.

Which currency should my home currency be?

The one you are paid in, usually AED. That keeps your monthly budget in the currency your salary arrives in.
